## Company background
NASA JSC has run the Robonaut program since 1997 to build dexterous humanoid torsos that can use astronaut tools and workstations unchanged. Robonaut 2, co-developed with General Motors under a 2007 Space Act Agreement, was unveiled in 2010 and flew to the ISS on STS-133 in February 2011 — the first humanoid robot in space [S1][S2]. GM's interest was industrial: dexterous-hand and sensor technology for manufacturing, which yielded the RoboGlove grip-assist exoskeleton glove, licensed to Sweden's Bioservo in 2016 [S3].

On Earth, R2 torsos were mounted on the four-wheeled Centaur 2 rover base (Desert RATS field trials 2010), a textbook wheeled semi-humanoid configuration [S2]. The ISS unit received climbing legs in 2014, but a power-supply fault (a missing return wire) left it largely disabled 2015-2018; it returned to Earth for repair in May 2018 and never re-flew. R2 is now displayed at the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum (2024) [S4][S5]. NASA's humanoid line continued with the bipedal Valkyrie (excluded from this study); the talent lineage runs to Nic Radford's Persona AI (Houston, welding humanoids).

## Relevance to the semi-humanoid market
R2 set the bar for dexterous robot hands (12-DoF, tendon-driven, tool-using) that commercial players still chase, and demonstrated the "torso on task-appropriate mobility" philosophy (pedestal on ISS, wheels as Centaur, later legs). As a competitor it is irrelevant (government, retired), but its technology diffusion — GM manufacturing robotics, RoboGlove/Bioservo, and the Radford alumni network — still shapes the US ecosystem an EU entrant faces.
