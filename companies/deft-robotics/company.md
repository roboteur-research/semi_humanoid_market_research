## Company background
Deft Robotics is a 2025-founded Korean-American startup that positions itself explicitly as a "systems integrator of wheeled humanoid robots" for automotive, appliance and electronics manufacturers. Its pitch targets the "last 20%" of factory processes that resist classic automation: line feeding, kitting, part alignment, cable insertion and flexible assembly [S1][S2]. Rather than mass-producing its own hardware, Deft partners with established robot manufacturers and concentrates on software, AI and integration — customers get a "drop-in workcell" without re-tooling the line [S2].

The AI approach is teleoperation-first: workers initially control the robots, generating video/sensor/state data that trains lightweight transformer models specialized for factory floors; humans remotely recover errors, and those recoveries feed retraining. A separate reward/failure-detection model flags edge cases, which are logged into the training set and shipped back via OTA model updates in maintenance windows [S1][S2]. Advertised performance: 75-95% of human speed from day one, 99.9% success in continuous 24-h operation, 6-9 month ROI, and a documented 96.77% placement success at 54.2 s/part in a production case [S1].

Pricing has evolved: the site advertised $40,000 per unit plus $30,000/year maintenance; the web store now lists "Simba: Medium Payload Unit" at $34,900 with 49 units sold, and founders have talked down the roadmap cost to <$17k (V2) and <$10k (V3) as assembly moves in-house [S4][S5]. Traction: 2 paid contracts with global enterprises, POCs/pilots with global automotive and electronics manufacturers, and a stated goal of 30 robots deployed within 12 months [S2][S3]. Published case studies cover compressor-housing and cross-cowl-bar line feeding for a Tier-1 US automotive components manufacturer [S1].

## Relevance to the semi-humanoid market
Deft is a credible early mover in the "wheeled humanoid as drop-in workcell" niche for automotive Tier-1s — exactly the segment a German entrant would target — with Korean OEM relationships (Hyundai lineage, SpringCamp backing) and a Detroit foothold. It is small (~12 people, seed stage) and hardware-light, so its threat is in software/integration speed and aggressive pricing rather than hardware depth. Trajectory: scaling pilots to fleet deployments; product documentation announced for Sept 2026.
